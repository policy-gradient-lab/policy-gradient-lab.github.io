1 Introduction

InstructGPT (Ouyang et al. 2022), a direct precursor to ChatGPT’s RLHF methodology, used Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al. 2017) with a KL-to-reference constraint to perform online policy optimization against a preference-trained reward model. PPO has since become a cornerstone of the GenAI era.

PPO is a policy-gradient method that updates a parameterized policy via stochastic gradient ascent to increase expected reward, but it is engineered to avoid the dominant failure mode of vanilla policy gradients: overly large steps that induce policy drift and abrupt performance collapse under noisy advantage estimates and scarce on-policy data. Its “proximal” idea is to constrain each update so the new policy cannot move too far from the old policy on sampled actions, keeping improvements incremental and reliable.

But to truly grasp how PPO calculates these updates and why it requires constraints, we must first understand the mathematical engine driving it: the fundamental Policy Gradient Theorem.

The Policy Gradient Theorem involves a lot of moving parts. Before we dive into the derivations, keep this notation glossary handy to help you track the variables, spaces, and probability distributions.

1.1 Spaces, States, and Actions

Notation Description
\mathcal{S} The state space (the set of all possible states in the environment).
\mathcal{A} The action space (the set of all possible actions the agent can take).
s, s', s'' Specific, concrete states within the environment.
S_t A random variable representing the state at a specific time step t.
s_t A specific, observed state value at time step t in a sample.
s_0, S_0 The initial state (s_0 as a concrete value, S_0 as a random variable).
a A specific action taken by the agent.
t A discrete time step in the environment.
\tau A realized trajectory or sampled sequence of interactions: \{s_0, a_0, r_1, s_1, \dots\}.

1.2 Functions and Parameters

Notation Description
\theta The learnable parameters of the neural network (or function approximator) defining the policy.
V^\pi(s) The state-value function; the expected return starting from state s and following policy \pi.
Q^\pi(s, a) The action-value function; the expected return starting from state s, taking action a, and then following policy \pi.
r(s, a) The immediate reward received by the agent after taking action a in state s.
J(\theta) The overall objective function to be maximized (the expected return of the policy).
\phi(s) The local policy improvement term at state s, defined as the sum of policy gradients weighted by action-values.

1.3 Probabilities and Distributions

Notation Description
\pi The policy followed by the agent.
\pi(a \mid s) The probability of taking action a when in state s, according to policy \pi.
P(s' \mid s, a) The environment’s transition probability of moving to state s' after taking action a in state s. This is the transition model (or system dynamics) of the Markov Decision Process (MDP). It belongs entirely to the environment and has absolutely nothing to do with the agent’s policy.
P(s \to s') The transition probability of moving from state s to s' under the current behavior of policy \pi. This is the state-to-state transition probability under a specific policy \pi. It combines the agent’s decision-making with the environment’s physics.
\mu_0(s_0) The probability distribution of the starting state s_0.
\mu_\pi(s) The discounted state visitation distribution (or unnormalized frequency) indicating how much time the agent spends in state s under policy \pi.
\text{Pr}(S_t = s \mid s_0, \pi) The exact probability of the agent being in state s at time step t, given it started at s_0 and follows policy \pi.

2 Bellman Equation

The Bellman Equation (Sutton and Barto 2018) for the state-value function V^\pi(s) is the expected value of all possible actions taken from that state:

V^\pi(s) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a|s) Q^\pi(s, a)

When we apply the gradient operator \nabla_\theta to both sides of this equation, we use the product rule (Leibniz rule) because both the policy \pi(a|s) and the action-value function Q^\pi(s, a) are functions of the policy parameters \theta:

\nabla_{\theta} V^{\pi}(s) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \left[ \nabla_{\theta} \pi(a|s) Q^{\pi}(s, a) + \pi(a|s) \nabla_{\theta} Q^{\pi}(s, a) \right] \tag{1}

The Bellman equation for the action-value function Q^\pi(s, a) is:

Q^{\pi}(s, a) = r(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}} P(s'|s, a) V^{\pi}(s')

This equation defines the value of taking a specific action a in state s as the immediate reward r(s, a) plus the discounted expected value of the resulting next state s'. When we apply the gradient operator \nabla_{\theta} to this expression, the immediate reward r(s, a) and the environment’s transition probabilities P(s'|s, a) are treated as constants because they do not depend on the policy parameters \theta.

Consequently, the gradient passes through the summation and the transition model, focusing entirely on the change in the future state-value function \nabla_{\theta} V^{\pi}(s'):

\nabla_{\theta} Q^{\pi}(s, a) = \nabla_{\theta} \left( r(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}} P(s'|s, a) V^{\pi}(s') \right) = \gamma \sum_{s' \in \mathcal{S}} P(s'|s, a) \nabla_{\theta} V^{\pi}(s') \tag{2}

Substituting Equation 2 back into the original expression Equation 1 reveals a recursive structure:

\nabla_{\theta} V^{\pi}(s) = \underbrace{\sum_{a} \nabla_{\theta} \pi(a|s) Q^{\pi}(s, a)}_{\phi(s)} + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s \to s') \nabla_{\theta} V^{\pi}(s') \tag{3} Here, P(s \to s') represents the transition probability from state s to s' under the current policy \pi. Replace the \nabla_\theta V^\pi(s') on the right side with its own definition Equation 3, we have: \nabla_\theta V^\pi(s) = \phi(s) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s \to s') \left[ \phi(s') + \gamma \sum_{s''} P(s' \to s'') \nabla_\theta V^\pi(s'') \right] By distributing the transition probability across the terms of the recursive expansion, the relationship between the state-space summations and the time-indexed expectations becomes clear:

\nabla_\theta V^\pi(s) = \phi(s) + \gamma \underbrace{\sum_{s'} P(s \to s') \phi(s')}_{\mathbb{E}_\pi[\phi(S_1) \mid s]} + \gamma^2 \underbrace{\sum_{s'} \sum_{s''} P(s \to s') P(s' \to s'') \phi(s'')}_{\mathbb{E}_\pi[\phi(S_2) \mid s]} + \dots \tag{4}

This expansion demonstrates how the value function gradient at state s is constructed from the immediate local improvement and the discounted sum of expected future improvements. The terms under the brackets identify that the summation over all possible next states s' is the mathematical definition of the expectation over the random variable S_1, while the nested summation over s'' represents the expectation over S_2. By viewing the derivation this way, it is evident that the gradient at any single point is a cumulative measure of all potential future policy updates along every possible path the agent might take.

Side note: To calculate P(s \to s'), you have to sum up the probabilities of every possible action the agent could take in state s, multiplied by the environment’s probability of moving to s' given that specific action. Mathematically, P(s \to s') is the marginalization of P(s' \mid s, a) over the policy \pi:

P(s \to s') = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a \mid s) P(s' \mid s, a) This shows exactly why P(s \to s') shifts whenever you update the policy parameters \theta, whereas P(s' \mid s, a) remains rigidly fixed for the entire training process!

3 From a Time-based View to a State-based View

The core “magic” of the policy gradient theorem is the conceptual shift from tracking an agent’s individual steps through time (t=0, 1, 2, \dots) to observing the aggregate density of the agent’s presence across the environment’s state space (s \in \mathcal{S}). It proves that we do not need to calculate an infinite series of future effects for every specific moment in time. Instead, we can simply look at the global state visitation distribution \mu_\pi(s)—the state-based view—to determine how to update our policy parameters \theta.

The time-based view (forward perspective) describes the gradient as the expected sum of all future local policy “nudges” \phi(S_t), discounted by \gamma^t, starting from the initial state s_0. It focuses on the sequential trajectory of the agent.

The state-based view (stationary perspective) collapses the time dimension into the stationary distribution \mu_\pi(s). It treats the gradient as a simple weighted average of the local improvements \phi(s) over the entire state space, which is what allows us to train using a buffer of sampled transitions.

To clarify the notation, we use S_t (uppercase) for the random variable and s_t (lowercase) for the specific value observed in a sample. The sampled sequence is a realized trajectory \tau = \{s_0, a_0, r_1, s_1, \dots\}.

3.1 The Time-based View

The goal of the agent is to maximize its expected return from the moment it begins interacting with the environment. We formally define this objective function J(\theta) as the expected value of the state-value function over the initial state distribution:

J(\theta) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{s_0 \sim \mu_0}[V^\pi(s_0)]

Writing this expectation explicitly as a sum over all possible initial states s_0, weighted by their starting probability \mu_0(s_0), gives us the starting point for our derivation:

J(\theta) = \sum_{s_0} \mu_0(s_0) V^{\pi}(s_0) Before moving further, it is worth pausing to clarify the notation \mu_0. The subscript 0 explicitly refers to the time step t=0, denoting the environment’s initial state distribution. This distinction is necessary because the very first state an agent encounters is typically determined entirely by the environment’s reset mechanics, independent of the agent’s behavior. We use the 0 subscript to isolate this localized starting point from the subsequent trajectory. This directly contrasts with \mu_\pi(s), which appears in the state-based view (stationary perspective). While \mu_0 only captures the isolated snapshot at the exact moment an episode begins, \mu_\pi(s) is the discounted state visitation distribution that aggregates the probability of visiting a state across all future time steps (t=0, 1, 2, \dots). Unlike the starting state, this global distribution is continuously shaped by the agent’s active policy \pi.

We begin with the gradient of the objective J(\theta), which is the weighted sum of the gradients of the values of the initial states s_0: \nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \sum_{s_0} \mu_0(s_0) \nabla_{\theta} V^{\pi}(s_0) As established in the recursive unrolling of the Bellman equation in Equation 4, \nabla_{\theta} V^{\pi}(s_0) is equal to the discounted sum of all future local policy improvements \phi(\cdot) that the agent will encounter: \nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \sum_{s_0} \mu_0(s_0) \left[ \phi(s_0) + \gamma \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\phi(S_1) \mid s_0] + \gamma^2 \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\phi(S_2) \mid s_0] + \dots \right] Note that s_0 is the specific value we are summing over. S_1 is the random variable (the unknown future). We are iterating through every possible concrete state in the state space \mathcal{S} and weighting the result by the probability \mu_0(s_0). When we are “inside” a summation, we are usually dealing with a specific, fixed value for that iteration.However, the starting point of the process, considered as a concept, is a random variable S_0.

3.2 The State-based View

To see how the “collapse” happens, we write out the expectations as explicit sums over all possible states s that could be visited at each time step t:

\nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \sum_{s_0} \mu_0(s_0) \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t \sum_{s} \text{Pr}(S_t = s \mid s_0, \pi) \phi(s)

The mathematical “miracle” occurs when we swap the order of the summations for t and s. Instead of tracking what happens at each second of the agent’s life, we look at each state s in the environment and ask how much total discounted weight it accumulates across all possible times: \nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \sum_{s} \left[ \sum_{s_0} \mu_0(s_0) \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t \text{Pr}(S_t = s \mid s_0, \pi) \right] \phi(s) \tag{5} The expression inside the brackets is exactly the definition of the discounted state visitation distribution \mu_\pi(s). It represents the total discounted probability of visiting state s over an infinite horizon, given the starting distribution \mu_0 and the behavior of policy \pi: \mu_\pi(s) = \sum_{s_0} \mu_0(s_0) \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t \text{Pr}(S_t = s \mid s_0, \pi) In the formula, S_t is the random variable representing the state at time t, and the sum over s_0 accounts for the probability of starting in various initial states according to the distribution \mu_0. This expression represents the unnormalized discounted state visitation frequency, which measures the total expected discounted time an agent spends in state s over an infinite horizon. Unlike a standard probability distribution that sums to one, the sum of this measure over the entire state space \mathcal{S} equals 1/(1-\gamma), reflecting the total discounted time available in the process. When using this unnormalized form in the policy gradient theorem, the gradient \nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) is expressed as a direct sum \sum_{s} \mu_{\pi}(s) \phi(s), effectively accumulating every local policy improvement throughout the agent’s lifetime. If a normalized probability distribution is required for an expectation, this frequency is typically multiplied by (1-\gamma) to ensure the weights sum to unity.

By substituting \mu_\pi(s) back into Equation 5, the infinite time series collapses into a single, elegant spatial weighting: \nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \sum_{s} \mu_\pi(s) \phi(s) \tag{6}

This result is fundamental because it proves that the complex, indirect effect of the policy on the future state distribution is already captured by simply weighting the local gradients by the current visitation frequency. We do not need to compute the derivative of the distribution itself; we only need to sample from it. As established in the recursive unrolling, the total gradient of the objective J(\theta) is the sum of these local improvements across all states, weighted by the discounted visitation frequency \mu_\pi(s).

4 Bringing It All Together: The Standard Policy Gradient

As shown in Equation 3, the local policy improvement term is defined by the following equation:

\phi(s) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \nabla_\theta \pi(a|s) Q^\pi(s, a) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a|s) \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi(a|s)}{\pi(a|s)} Q^\pi(s, a) \text{}

Recognizing that \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi(a|s)}{\pi(a|s)} = \nabla_\theta \log \pi(a|s), we can rewrite the summation as an expectation under the current policy:

\phi(s) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a|s) Q^\pi(s, a) \nabla_\theta \log \pi(a|s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi} [Q^\pi(s, a) \nabla_\theta \log \pi(a|s) ] \text{} By substituting \phi(s) back into Equation 6, the total gradient formula now looks like this: \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_s \mu_\pi(s) \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi} [Q^\pi(s, a) \nabla_\theta \log \pi(a|s)] Because \mu_\pi(s) represents the discounted probability distribution of states under policy \pi, the summation over s can be rewritten as an expectation over states sampled from that distribution: \nabla_\theta J(\theta) \propto \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \mu_\pi} [ \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi} [Q^\pi(s, a) \nabla_\theta \log \pi(a|s)] ]

By combining the nested expectations, we arrive at the standard policy gradient form (Sutton and Barto 2018) : \nabla_\theta J(\theta) \propto \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \mu_\pi, a \sim \pi} [Q^\pi(s, a) \nabla_\theta \log \pi(a|s)] The proportionality symbol (\propto) is used because \mu_\pi(s) is often defined as a frequency \eta(s) that requires normalization by (1-\gamma) to become a proper probability distribution that sums to 1. In practice, this normalization is absorbed into the learning rate during optimization.

5 Conclusion

The policy gradient theorem establishes that \nabla_\theta J(\theta) can be computed without explicitly deriving the gradient of the state distribution \nabla_\theta \mu_{\pi_\theta}. While it is true that \mu_\pi(s) is coupled with the policy \pi and shifts whenever \theta is updated, the theorem demonstrates that these complex environmental dependencies are absorbed into the expectation over the discounted state visitation distribution. This allows the objective J(\theta) to be optimized using only the local policy gradient \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) and the advantage, effectively side-stepping the need to model unknown transition dynamics.

However, this shortcut only holds true for the exact gradient at the current \theta; as soon as the policy moves too far, the old distribution \mu_{\pi_{old}}(s) no longer matches the new reality, causing the gradient estimate to become unreliable. Understanding this limitation naturally leads to the next question: how can we update policies while ensuring the new policy does not drift too far from the old one? This question motivates Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO), which we explore in the next post: LLM Alignment Series: Trust Region Policy Optimization.
