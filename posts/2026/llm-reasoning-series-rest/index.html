Reinforcement learning from human feedback aligns a language model with human preferences by training a reward model on preference data and then optimizing the policy against that reward. The standard optimizer for this step is an online policy-gradient method such as PPO (Schulman et al. 2017), used in InstructGPT and related systems (Ouyang et al. 2022). Online optimization has two costs. First, it is sample-inefficient in a specific way: every gradient step requires fresh samples drawn from the current policy and scored by the reward model, so the generation and scoring cost is paid continuously and grows with the size of both networks. Second, an online policy can chase weaknesses in the reward model — assigning high reward to outputs the model does not actually prefer — a failure the literature calls reward hacking.

ReST is a method that removes the online sampling loop while keeping the ability to improve on self-generated data (Gulcehre et al. 2023). It frames alignment as a growing-batch reinforcement learning problem and splits it into two stages that run separately. A Grow step samples a large batch of outputs from the current policy and scores them with the reward model, producing a fixed dataset. An Improve step then fine-tunes the policy on the subset of that dataset whose reward exceeds a threshold, and repeats with a higher threshold each time. Because the expensive generation happens once per Grow step and is reused across many Improve steps, the per-update cost of generation is amortized. Because the dataset is fixed and stored, it can be inspected before training, which makes reward-model exploitation easier to detect than in an online loop where data is consumed as it is produced. The paper studies ReST on machine translation, where reliable reference-free reward models exist, and reports gains over a supervised baseline in both automated reward and human evaluation, at lower compute than online RL.

1 Conditional Language Modeling and the Behavioral Cloning Baseline

The setting is conditional generation. Given a context \mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_L) — for translation, a source sentence — an autoregressive policy \pi_\theta defines a distribution over output sequences \mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_T) by factoring it token by token:

\pi_\theta(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \pi_\theta\!\left(y_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}, \mathbf{x}\right).

Given a dataset \mathcal{D} of context–target pairs drawn from a data distribution p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = p(\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}), the initial policy is trained by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the targets:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi_\theta\!\left(y_t \mid \mathbf{y}_{1:t-1}, \mathbf{x}\right)\right].

The paper refers to a model trained with this loss as behavioral cloning (BC), following reinforcement-learning nomenclature: the model imitates the demonstrations in \mathcal{D} without reference to any reward. BC is the starting point for ReST and also the baseline it is measured against. In the paper’s naming convention, this baseline is written \texttt{BC}\ (\texttt{G=0, I=0}) — zero Grow steps and zero Improve steps, trained only on the original data.

The underlying control problem can be cast as a Markov decision process in which a state s_n = [\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}_{1:n-1}] is the source plus the partial output, an action a_n = y_n is the next token, and the transition is the deterministic concatenation of that token onto the sequence. The reward is sparse: the reward model scores only the complete sequence, emitted after the end-of-sequence token, and is zero at every intermediate step. The authors note this is equivalently a contextual bandit with a very large action space. The determinism matters for a later design choice, because it limits how much value-based offline RL can gain over plain imitation.

2 The Grow Step: Sampling an Augmented Dataset

The Grow step is the data-generation, or acting, stage. Using the current policy \pi_\theta, the procedure samples output sequences for contexts drawn from the original dataset and scores each with the reward function R(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}). The result is an augmented dataset

\mathcal{D}_g = \Big\{\, (\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)\big|_{i=1}^{N_g} \ \text{ s.t. }\ \mathbf{x}^i \sim \mathcal{D},\ \mathbf{y}^i \sim \pi_\theta(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}^i) \,\Big\} \cup \mathcal{D}.

Two design choices in this definition are worth stating explicitly. The contexts \mathbf{x}^i are resampled from the original dataset, exactly as in self-training; where the marginal p(\mathbf{x}) is directly available the contexts could be sampled from it instead, but in translation the source sentences come from \mathcal{D}. And the union with \mathcal{D} keeps the original human references in the training mixture rather than replacing them with model samples. Retaining the references is a guard against the policy drifting away from the data distribution as it trains on its own outputs; the role of that term becomes precise in the gradient analysis below.

Sampling uses a tempered softmax at temperature 0.8, chosen because it produces outputs spanning a broad range of rewards. A wide reward spread is what the Improve step needs: if every sample scored about the same, thresholding would not separate better outputs from worse ones.

3 The Improve Step: Reward Filtering and a Reward-Weighted Loss

The Improve step is policy improvement. It selects the high-reward portion of \mathcal{D}_g and fine-tunes on it. Selection is done by an indicator filtering function with threshold \tau,

F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\tau) = \mathbb{1}_{\,R(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) > \tau},

which is 1 when the reward clears the threshold and 0 otherwise. The policy is then trained on the reward-weighted objective

J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_g}\!\left[\, F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\tau)\,\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\theta) \,\right],

where \mathcal{L} is a per-example loss, which may be the NLL loss above or an offline RL loss.

Why a hard indicator rather than weighting each example by its reward? The indicator makes the per-step loss reward-agnostic given that the example passed the filter; all of the reward information enters through which examples are kept. This keeps the inner optimization identical to ordinary supervised fine-tuning — the only machinery added to a standard language-model pipeline is a precomputed score and a comparison — and it is what allows the best-performing configuration to use the plain NLL loss for \mathcal{L} while still being driven by reward. The paper does flag a known caveat: hard threshold filtering can favor high-variance outcomes in stochastic environments (Gulcehre et al. 2023), but the translation tasks here are deterministic, so this does not arise.

4 Increasing Thresholds Across Improve Steps

A single Grow step is followed by several Improve steps, and the threshold rises at each one: \tau_1 < \tau_2 < \cdots < \tau_N. The experiments draw thresholds from the increasing sequence [\,0.0,\,0.7,\,0.8,\,0.9,\,0.95,\,0.99\,] on rewards normalized to [0,1], with the \tau = 0.0 case corresponding to training on the full augmented dataset.

Each increase shrinks the surviving subset and raises its average quality, so the policy is fine-tuned on progressively cleaner but smaller data. The first threshold is deliberately set above the current policy’s mean reward, \tau_1 > V_{\pi_\theta} where V_{\pi_\theta} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_g}[R(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]; training only on samples above the current mean is what makes each step an improvement rather than a re-fit of the same distribution. The appendix states the monotonicity directly: filtering with a threshold at or above the current value V^{\pi_0} and fitting a new policy \pi^1 yields V^{\pi_1} > V^{\pi_0}, so the increasing-threshold schedule produces a sequence of improving policies.

Two practical measures keep this from degenerating. Because the high-threshold subsets are small and large models overfit small data quickly, each new policy is fine-tuned from the previous one with a lower learning rate rather than retrained from scratch. And because sampling in the Grow step is the expensive operation, multiple Improve steps are run on each generated dataset, amortizing the one-time generation cost over many cheap supervised updates. The number of Improve steps is dataset-dependent: the paper runs five on IWSLT 2014, four on WMT 2020, and two on Web Domain.

5 Why the Improve Step Is a Policy Gradient

The reward-weighted objective is presented as supervised fine-tuning on filtered data, but its gradient connects it to reinforcement learning. Take \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}, let \theta' be the parameters of the model that generated the data in the last Grow step, and let \lambda be the fraction of \mathcal{D}_g drawn from that model (the rest being the original data). For a single Grow step the gradient is

\nabla J(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\, \lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\pi_{\theta'}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})}\!\left[F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\tau)\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\right] + (1-\lambda)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})}\!\left[F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\tau)\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\right]\Big].

The two terms have distinct roles. The first term is an expectation over samples from the generating policy \pi_{\theta'}. At the start of training, when \theta \approx \theta', it has the form of an online policy gradient \mathbb{E}[\,Q^\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\,] with the filter F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\tau) standing in for the state-action value Q^\pi. This is the sense in which ReST performs reinforcement learning: a binary “did this clear the bar” signal replaces the value estimate that an online algorithm would compute, and the policy is pushed toward the trajectories that cleared it. The approximation holds only near the start because the data were generated by \pi_{\theta'}, not the current \pi_\theta; as \theta moves away from \theta' the term becomes off-policy, which is why a fresh Grow step is eventually needed.

The second term is the same gradient but over the original data p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}), still gated by the filter. It is an offline policy-gradient term that anchors \pi_\theta to the data distribution. This is the formal reason the Grow step keeps \mathcal{D} in the mixture: the (1-\lambda) term prevents the policy from moving too far from p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}), which the paper connects to avoiding model collapse — the degradation that occurs when a model is trained repeatedly on its own generations with the original data removed. The same construction also makes explicit a criterion that is usually implicit: the human references in \mathcal{D} were themselves selected by some unstated quality standard, and applying F to them as well states that standard rather than assuming it.

The appendix gives a population-level reading of the whole loop, valid as the sample counts grow without bound. The initial BC step minimizes \mathrm{KL}\big(p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\big), which reduces to \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[\mathrm{KL}(p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}))\big]. Each Improve step then defines a filtered target distribution proportional to the reward-thresholded mixture of original and synthetic data,

\pi^{\tau_i}_{\theta_k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) \ \propto\ \big\{(1-\lambda)\,p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) + \lambda\,\pi_{\theta_k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\big\}\,\mathbb{1}\big(R(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) > \tau_i\big),

and projects the policy onto it by minimizing \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi^{\tau_i}_{\theta_k} \,\|\, \pi_\theta\big), raising the threshold until the expected reward stops increasing. In this view each Improve step is a KL projection onto a reweighted, reward-filtered version of the current data, and the increasing threshold sequence is a schedule of progressively more selective targets.

6 Experiments on Machine Translation

Translation is the testbed because it has established reference-free reward models. The experiments use Metric X (Gulcehre et al. 2023) as the reward, scoring a candidate from the source alone without a human reference. Reference-free scoring is chosen deliberately: a reference-based model assigns higher scores to candidates resembling the reference, which penalizes valid translations that differ from it and caps quality at the reference’s own, sometimes erroneous, level. The cost of dropping the reference is greater exposure to distribution shift and reward hacking, since the score is not anchored to a human target; the authors mitigate this with hand-built “unit tests” of the reward model and report it remained imperfect, occasionally raising a translation’s score simply when a sentence was repeated.

Three benchmarks with different language pairs test generality: IWSLT 2014 German–English, WMT 2020 Chinese–English, and an internal Web Domain English–Chinese set. Results are reported as average reward on held-out sentences. Variants are named by loss, Grow count, and Improve count, e.g. \texttt{GOLD G=1 I=2}; when the loss is unspecified the BC (NLL) loss is used.

The first finding is that each Improve step raises the validation reward on all three datasets. With a single Grow step and the increasing-threshold schedule, every additional Improve step improves the reward model score, and the useful number of steps is dataset-dependent. The second finding is that a second Grow step — regenerating data from the improved policy, then running further Improve steps — adds further gains: an improvement of 5.3 points on IWSLT 2014 and 0.8 points on Web Domain over the end of the first Grow step. Across datasets and language pairs, every ReST variant exceeds the supervised BC baseline, even after only the first Grow step.

On the choice of inner loss, the paper compares offline actor–critic, Behavior V-MPO, GOLD, and BC inside the Improve loop and finds the BC loss best for maximizing the reward model score. The result is initially counterintuitive, because BC normally ignores reward entirely. In ReST the reward enters BC through the filtering stage: for I \geq 1 the BC loss is applied only to samples that passed the threshold, so reward shapes the training set even though it does not appear in the loss. The discussion attributes the underperformance of the value-based offline RL losses to the difficulty of learning value functions under sparse rewards and a 32,000-token action space, together with the deterministic dynamics, which leave little room for a value-based method to improve over imitation.

ReST also composes with Best-of-N sampling at inference time, where N samples are drawn and the reward model selects the top-ranked one. Both the baseline and ReST benefit from larger N, and the gains stack with the number of Improve steps: the best ReST variant with N < 10 matches the BC model at N = 200. After three Improve steps with N = 200, ReST reaches the maximum reward of 1, scoring above the reference translations in \mathcal{D} — a result that also shows ReST has not collapsed sample diversity to the point where Best-of-N stops helping.

7 Comparison with Online RL and the Reward-Hacking Signal

The comparison against PPO is run with the online method given a comparable amount of training data to ReST with one Grow step (Gulcehre et al. 2023). On IWSLT 2014:

Algorithm Average Reward Distinct samples
BC (G=0, I=0) 70.9 16,000,000
ReST (G=1, I=0) 71.9 16,000,000
ReST (G=1, I=4) 77.8 16,000,000
ReST (G=2, I=3) 83.1 32,000,000
Online RL (PPO) 71.6 24,000,000

Online RL lands at 71.6, about equal to ReST with one Grow step and no Improve steps — which is itself just BC on the generated dataset \mathcal{D}_g — while consuming more distinct samples. Once the Improve steps are added, ReST pulls well ahead at the same data budget, reaching 83.1 with a second Grow step.

The reward numbers come with a diagnostic. The PPO policy’s BLEU score on the validation set dropped by nearly 8 points while its reward rose, whereas ReST’s BLEU did not change. A reward that climbs while an independent quality metric falls is the signature of reward hacking: the policy is exploiting the reward model rather than improving translation. That the decoupled, inspectable ReST pipeline did not show this divergence, while the online loop did, is the paper’s concrete evidence for the claim that separating data generation from training lowers the “alignment tax.”

8 Where the Reward Model Stops Being a Proxy

A final result tempers the others. Human raters scored ReST and BC translations on a 0–6 scale, and every ReST variant beat BC in human preference. But the ranking by human score did not match the ranking by reward model score, and the gap widens with more Grow and Improve steps. The interpretation offered is that the learned reward model is an imperfect proxy that generalizes worse as the policy moves away from the behavior model — exactly the regime that repeated Grow and Improve steps produce. Continued optimization eventually overfits the proxy: reward keeps rising while the quantity it stands in for does not. On human evaluation, a single Grow step is the best operating point, even though automated reward continues to climb past it.

This points to where the method’s limits sit. ReST converts the online RLHF loop into a sequence of offline supervised fits on reward-filtered self-generated data, which is cheaper, more stable, and easier to inspect, and which improves a fixed reward monotonically. None of that repairs the reward model itself. The paper suggests retraining the reward model between Grow steps on human annotations of the latest policy’s outputs — keeping the proxy aligned with the moving policy — as the way to keep optimizing without overfitting, and leaves it as future work. ReST applies wherever a robust reward model and scalable sampling are both available, which the authors note extends beyond translation to summarization, dialogue, and other generative settings.
