Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) (Schulman, Levine, et al. 2017) is a policy-based reinforcement learning method that shares the same fundamental objective function as standard policy gradient algorithms: maximizing the expected value J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_S[V_\pi(S)].

As shown by the Policy Gradient Theorem (see my previous post LLM Alignment Series: The Policy Gradient Theorem), this objective can be optimized by estimating the gradient of J(\theta) with respect to the policy parameters. Importantly, TRPO does not change this objective; instead, it modifies how the policy update is performed by constraining how far the new policy can move from the old one. While standard policy gradient algorithms rely on stochastic gradient ascent to improve the policy, TRPO utilizes a trust-region algorithm. This approach ensures more stable and reliable updates by constraining how much the policy can change in a single step, helping to prevent the performance collapses often seen with traditional gradient methods. In the next post LLM Alignment Series: Proximal Policy Optimization, we will see how Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) builds on the same idea but replaces the trust-region constraint with a simpler clipped objective (Schulman, Wolski, et al. 2017) that is easier to implement in modern deep learning pipelines.

1 Trust Region Optimization

At its core, Trust Region optimization is a strategy for safely navigating complex, non-linear objective functions f(x) by relying on bounded local approximations. Instead of picking a direction and then guessing a step size like standard line search methods, trust region methods first define a safe maximum step distance, and then find the optimal update within that strict boundary.

The algorithm proceeds in three main phases:

  1. The Local Approximation

    At the current parameter state x_k, we construct a simplified local model of the landscape, typically a second-order Taylor expansion denoted as m_k(p), where p is the proposed step vector. We mathematically “trust” this approximation is accurate only within a specific radius, \Delta_k.

  2. The Constrained Subproblem

    We find the optimal step p by minimizing the local model subject to the trust-region boundary constraint:

    Minimize: m_k(p)

    Subject to: \|p\| \le \Delta_k

  3. The Dynamic Evaluation

    Before officially updating the parameters, we measure how well our local model predicted the actual landscape. We calculate the agreement ratio \rho_k, which compares the actual reduction in the true objective function to the predicted reduction from our simplified model:

    \rho_k = \frac{f(x_k) - f(x_k + p_k)}{m_k(0) - m_k(p_k)}

Based on \rho_k, the algorithm dynamically adjusts. If \rho_k is close to 1, the model is highly accurate; the step is accepted, and the trust region \Delta_k is expanded for faster optimization. If \rho_k is negative, the model was wrong and the step actually made things worse; the step is rejected, the trust region is shrunk, and the algorithm tries again within a tighter, safer boundary. This feedback loop guarantees stable, monotonic improvement even in highly unpredictable loss landscapes.

2 Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)

2.1 The Surrogate Objective

Policy Gradient Theorem proves that the gradient of the state distribution does not affect the policy gradient at the current parameters \theta, it saves us from modeling the environment’s transition dynamics. This shortcut only holds true for the exact gradient at the current \theta. This is exactly why TRPO cannot just use J(\theta) and must rely on the surrogate objective L(\theta).

Instead of directly maximizing the expected return J(\theta), TRPO optimizes a surrogate objective function. Let \theta_{old} be the parameters of the current policy and \theta be the parameters of the proposed new policy. TRPO seeks to maximize the following expected advantage:

L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s, a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)} A_{\theta_{old}}(s, a) \right]

In this equation, A_{\theta_{old}}(s, a) is the advantage function, which measures how much better a specific action is compared to the average baseline action in a given state. The ratio \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)} tracks how the probability of taking that action changes under the new policy.

It was proved that the true objective J(\theta) is mathematically guaranteed to improve as long as L(\theta) improves and the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the old and new policies is strictly penalized (Schulman, Levine, et al. 2017) . By enforcing a hard constraint on this KL divergence, TRPO traps the optimization step inside a safe, narrow boundary where L(\theta) remains a valid and reliable proxy for J(\theta).

2.2 The KL Divergence Constraint

To prevent the catastrophic updates mentioned earlier, TRPO restricts the change in the policy using Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, a mathematical statistic that measures how one probability distribution differs from a second, reference probability distribution.

Rather than just adding a penalty to the objective function, TRPO enforces a hard constraint on the average KL divergence over the state space. The formal constrained optimization problem is written as:

Maximize:

L(\theta) \tag{1}

Subject to:

\mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|s) \parallel \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|s)) \right] \le \delta

The parameter \delta is a predefined, small scalar value that defines the maximum allowed KL divergence. This creates the “trust region” around the current policy. By enforcing this boundary, the algorithm mathematically guarantees that the new policy \pi_{\theta} will not diverge significantly from \pi_{\theta_{old}} for any given state. Because the policy is only allowed to change within this safe, trusted region, TRPO maintains stable and theoretically guaranteed monotonic improvement during the learning process.

2.3 Local Approximation using Taylor Expansion

Let the change in policy parameters be \Delta \theta = \theta - \theta_{old}.

First, we approximate the objective function L(\theta) using a first-order Taylor expansion around \theta_{old}:

L(\theta) \approx L(\theta_{old}) + g^T \Delta \theta

Here, g is the policy gradient evaluated at \theta_{old}:

g = \nabla_{\theta} L(\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}}

Second, we approximate the KL divergence constraint using a second-order Taylor expansion. The zeroth and first-order terms of the KL divergence between identical distributions are zero, leaving only the second-order term. The detailed proof is provided in Section 4.

\bar{D}_{KL}(\theta_{old}, \theta) \approx \frac{1}{2} \Delta \theta^T H \Delta \theta

Here, H is the Hessian matrix of the KL divergence. In the context of probability distributions, this Hessian is equivalent to the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM), as shown in Section 5.

\bar{D}_{KL}(\theta_{old}, \theta) is a shorthand convention used heavily in the actual TRPO paper to make the equations readable. The bar on top denotes a mean or expectation. It specifically represents the average KL divergence over all the states actually sampled in the rollout buffer. Mathematically, it is defined as: \bar{D}_{KL}(\theta_{old}, \theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|s) \parallel \pi_\theta(\cdot|s)) \right] D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|s) \parallel \pi_\theta(\cdot|s)) is the exact, formal application of that concept to our policy networks. It represents the KL divergence between the old policy and the new policy at one single, specific state s.

By substituting these approximations back into the original TRPO formulation Equation 1, the problem simplifies into a quadratic program:

Maximize:

g^T \Delta \theta

Subject to:

\frac{1}{2} \Delta \theta^T H \Delta \theta \le \delta

To find the optimal step direction and size, we construct the Lagrangian for this constrained problem:

\mathcal{L}(\Delta \theta, \lambda) = g^T \Delta \theta - \lambda \left( \frac{1}{2} \Delta \theta^T H \Delta \theta - \delta \right)

We find the maximum by taking the derivative with respect to \Delta \theta and setting it to zero:

\nabla_{\Delta \theta} \mathcal{L} = g - \lambda H \Delta \theta = 0

\Delta \theta = \frac{1}{\lambda} H^{-1} g

This gives us the optimal update direction. Let the search direction be x = H^{-1} g. To find the scalar \lambda, we plug \Delta \theta back into the constraint boundary equation:

\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{\lambda} x \right)^T H \left( \frac{1}{\lambda} x \right) = \delta

\frac{1}{2 \lambda^2} x^T H x = \delta

\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{x^T H x}{2\delta}}

Finally, substituting \lambda back into the equation for \Delta \theta gives the exact parameter update:

\Delta \theta = \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{x^T H x}} x

2.4 Bypassing the Matrix Inversion with Conjugate Gradient

The equation above requires computing x = H^{-1} g, which is equivalent to solving the linear system Hx = g.

For modern deep learning architectures, the Fisher Information Matrix H is an N \times N matrix where N is the number of network parameters. Explicitly computing, storing, and inverting this matrix is computationally intractable.

This is where the conjugate gradient (CG) method comes in. CG is an iterative algorithm for solving systems of linear equations like Hx = g without ever needing to explicitly construct the matrix H. The algorithm only requires the ability to compute matrix-vector products, specifically Hv for an arbitrary vector v.

This operation is called a Hessian-vector product (HVP). Using automatic differentiation frameworks, the HVP can be computed efficiently with two backward passes, requiring only slightly more computation than standard backpropagation:

Hv = \nabla_{\theta} \left( \left( \nabla_{\theta} \bar{D}_{KL}(\theta_{old}, \theta) \right)^T v \right)

By using the conjugate gradient method to estimate the search direction x, and then scaling it by the constraint scalar, TRPO achieves stable policy updates without the massive computational overhead of matrix inversion.

2.5 The Backtracking Algorithm

The reason TRPO needs a backtracking line search is that the math we just worked through relies heavily on local approximations. We used a first-order Taylor expansion for the objective function and a second-order approximation for the KL divergence.

To bridge the gap between the quadratic approximation and the true non-linear reality of the neural network, TRPO uses the backtracking line search. It evaluates the exact functions to guarantee the step is strictly beneficial.

Here is the step-by-step mechanics of the search:

  1. Calculate the maximal proposed parameter update using the direction and scalar we derived earlier:

    \Delta \theta = \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{x^T H x}} x

  2. Define a decay coefficient \alpha \in (0, 1), often set to 0.5, and initialize a decay index j = 0.

  3. Propose a new set of policy parameters by taking a exponentially decaying fraction of the maximal step:

    \theta_{new} = \theta_{old} + \alpha^j \Delta \theta

  4. Evaluate this proposed \theta_{new} against two strict acceptance criteria using the true, unapproximated functions:

    Objective improvement: L(\theta_{new}) > L(\theta_{old})

    KL constraint boundary: D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}} \parallel \pi_{\theta_{new}}) \le \delta

  5. If both conditions are satisfied, the search successfully terminates, and \theta_{new} is accepted as the new policy.

  6. If either condition fails, it means the local approximation was too aggressive and overshot the safe region. The algorithm increments j by 1 (which shrinks the step size by another factor of \alpha) and repeats the evaluation.

2.6 Bridging the Gap with the Surrogate Objective

To understand why we can optimize a surrogate objective L(\theta) instead of the true expected return J(\theta), we need to look at their first derivatives. The foundational trick of TRPO is proving that at the exact point of the current policy parameters \theta_{old}, the gradient of the surrogate perfectly matches the gradient of the true objective.

Let’s define our terms formally:

  • \pi_\theta(a|s): The policy parameterized by \theta.

  • \theta_{old}: The current parameters of our policy before the update.

  • \rho_{\theta_{old}}(s): The state visitation distribution under the old policy.

  • A_{\theta_{old}}(s, a): The advantage function evaluated under the old policy.

First, we recall the standard Policy Gradient Theorem for our true objective J(\theta). Evaluated at the current parameters \theta_{old}, it is written as:

\nabla_\theta J(\theta)|_{\theta = \theta_{old}} = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho_{\theta_{old}}, a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)|_{\theta = \theta_{old}} A_{\theta_{old}}(s, a) \right]

Next, we define our surrogate objective L(\theta). This function uses importance sampling to evaluate a proposed new policy using the trajectories collected by the old policy:

L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho_{\theta_{old}}, a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)} A_{\theta_{old}}(s, a) \right]

Notice the fraction in the L(\theta) equation: \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)}. This is an importance sampling ratio. Because we do not know the state distribution for the proposed new policy \pi_\theta, TRPO evaluates the objective using the trajectories and state distribution already collected by the old policy \pi_{\theta_{old}}. The importance sampling ratio corrects for the fact that we are evaluating a new policy using data generated by an old one. It asks: if the new policy makes a good action more likely, how much should we increase the objective?

To find the gradient of this surrogate objective L(\theta), we take the derivative with respect to \theta. Because the expectation is taken over states and actions sampled from the old policy, the distribution over which we are taking the expectation is independent of the active variable \theta. This allows us to pass the gradient operator directly inside the expectation:

\nabla_\theta L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho_{\theta_{old}}, a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)} A_{\theta_{old}}(s, a) \right]

Now, we evaluate this gradient at the exact point of our current parameters, setting \theta = \theta_{old}:

\nabla_\theta L(\theta)|_{\theta = \theta_{old}} = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho_{\theta_{old}}, a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a|s)|_{\theta = \theta_{old}}}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)} A_{\theta_{old}}(s, a) \right]

At this stage, we apply the standard log-derivative trick, which states that \nabla_x \log f(x) = \frac{\nabla_x f(x)}{f(x)}. If we apply this identity to our policy at the point \theta_{old}, we get:

\frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a|s)|_{\theta = \theta_{old}}}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)} = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)|_{\theta = \theta_{old}}

Substituting this identity back into our expectation yields our final result:

\nabla_\theta L(\theta)|_{\theta = \theta_{old}} = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho_{\theta_{old}}, a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)|_{\theta = \theta_{old}} A_{\theta_{old}}(s, a) \right]

This final equation is identical to the right-hand side of the Policy Gradient Theorem. Therefore, we have proven:

\nabla_\theta L(\theta)|_{\theta = \theta_{old}} = \nabla_\theta J(\theta)|_{\theta = \theta_{old}}

Because their first derivatives match, taking a small step to maximize the easy-to-calculate surrogate L(\theta) guarantees we are moving in the exact same direction as if we were maximizing the impossible-to-calculate true objective J(\theta). However, because this equality only holds strictly at \theta = \theta_{old}, we must introduce the trust region constraint to ensure we do not step too far away from this point of equivalence.

2.7 Why TRPO Uses the Fisher Information Matrix

When we use standard gradient descent to update a neural network, we are taking a step in Euclidean space. We change the parameters \theta by some small Euclidean distance \Delta \theta.

However, a neural network policy \pi_\theta(a|s) outputs a probability distribution. The relationship between the parameters and the output distribution is highly non-linear. In some regions of the parameter space, a massive change in \theta might barely affect the output probabilities. In other regions, a microscopic change in \theta might drastically shift the probabilities, completely changing the agent’s behavior and destroying the policy.

If we define our trust region simply by limiting the Euclidean distance \Delta \theta^T \Delta \theta < \epsilon, we are ignoring the actual effect on the policy. Standard gradient descent does not understand the underlying geometry of the probability distributions it is creating.

The Fisher Information Matrix solves this problem by acting as a metric tensor for probability distributions. It defines the local geometry of the probability space, often referred to as a Riemannian manifold.

Instead of measuring the Euclidean distance between parameter vectors, we want to measure the statistical distance between the resulting probability distributions. The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence does exactly this.

If we look at the second-order Taylor expansion of the KL divergence between a policy and a slightly updated policy, a beautiful mathematical property emerges. The Hessian matrix (the matrix of second derivatives) of the KL divergence evaluated at the current parameters is exactly equal to the Fisher Information Matrix, which we will denote as F:

D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta} \parallel \pi_{\theta + \Delta \theta}) \approx \frac{1}{2} \Delta \theta^T F \Delta \theta

The FIM translates a change in parameters (\Delta \theta) into the resulting change in the probability distribution. By incorporating the FIM, TRPO transforms the standard gradient into the natural gradient. This ensures the algorithm takes steps of a consistent size in the actual behavior space, moving safely regardless of how the neural network parameters are parameterized.

TRPO explicitly substitutes the FIM into its optimization constraint to enforce the trust region. Recall the quadratic program TRPO attempts to solve. It maximizes the surrogate advantage g^T \Delta \theta subject to the KL divergence constraint. Using the relationship established above, TRPO mathematically frames the constraint using the FIM:

\frac{1}{2} \Delta \theta^T F \Delta \theta \le \delta

As derived earlier using Lagrange multipliers, the optimal step direction x for this constrained problem requires inverting this matrix:

x = F^{-1} g

This vector x is the natural gradient direction. It points toward the steepest improvement in the objective function while accounting for the curvature of the probability space defined by F.

Because the FIM is an N \times N matrix where N is the number of network parameters, explicitly constructing and inverting F is impossible for deep neural networks. TRPO uses the conjugate gradient method to approximate the solution to Fx = g. In this process, TRPO never builds the full FIM. Instead, it computes Fisher-vector products (Fv) for arbitrary vectors v.

Mathematically, the FIM is also defined as the expected outer product of the score function (the gradient of the log-probability):

F = \mathbb{E}_{s,a} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)^T \right]

By leveraging automatic differentiation packages, TRPO can compute the product of this massive matrix and a vector v efficiently using a sequence of vector-Jacobian products, providing the precise directional curvature needed for the natural gradient step without the impossible memory requirements.

2.8 The Monotonic Improvement Guarantee

We established that maximizing the surrogate objective L(\theta) is equivalent to maximizing the true objective J(\theta) exactly at the current parameters \theta_{old}. However, as the new policy \pi_\theta diverges from \pi_{\theta_{old}}, this approximation breaks down. If we take too large a step, an increase in L(\theta) might actually result in a catastrophic decrease in the true expected return.

To prevent this, Schulman et al. derived a theoretical lower bound that strictly bounds the difference between the true objective and the surrogate objective. They proved that the true expected return is mathematically guaranteed to be greater than or equal to the surrogate objective minus a penalty based on how much the policy distribution changes.

The theoretical lower bound is defined as:

J(\theta) \ge L(\theta) - C \max_s D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|s) \parallel \pi_\theta(\cdot|s))

Let’s break down the components of this inequality:

  • J(\theta): The true expected return of the proposed new policy.

  • L(\theta): The surrogate objective evaluated using the old policy’s state distribution, \rho_{\theta_{old}}(s).

  • \max_s D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|s) \parallel \pi_\theta(\cdot|s)): The maximum Kullback-Leibler divergence between the old and new policy across all possible states s.

  • C: A theoretical penalty coefficient defined as C = \frac{4\epsilon \gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}, where \epsilon is the maximum absolute advantage and \gamma is the discount factor.

This equation is the mathematical bedrock of Trust Region Policy Optimization. It provides an absolute safety net. If we can find a new policy \theta that maximizes the right-hand side of this inequality, we are mathematically guaranteed that the true objective J(\theta) on the left-hand side will improve by at least that same amount.

By forcing the optimization process to consider the KL divergence penalty, the algorithm is mathematically prevented from taking a step that destroys the policy. It guarantees monotonic improvement.

3 From Natural Policy Gradient to Trust Region Policy Optimization

Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) is fundamentally an extension and practical stabilization of the Natural Policy Gradient (NPG) algorithm (Kakade 2001). While they share the exact same underlying philosophy regarding the geometry of probability space, TRPO solves a critical flaw in how NPG applies its updates.

Both NPG and TRPO recognize that standard gradient descent is flawed for updating policies. Taking a step of a fixed Euclidean distance in parameter space can lead to wildly unpredictable changes in the actual probability distribution of the policy.

To fix this, both algorithms calculate the exact same update direction. They multiply the standard policy gradient g by the inverse of the Fisher Information Matrix F:

x = F^{-1} g

This vector x is the natural gradient. It points in the direction of steepest objective improvement while accounting for the curvature of the probability manifold.

3.1 Where NPG Falls Short: The Step Size Problem

The original Natural Policy Gradient algorithm uses this natural gradient direction but relies on a standard, fixed learning rate (or step size) \alpha:

\theta_{new} = \theta_{old} + \alpha F^{-1} g

This is where NPG runs into practical problems. Because the curvature of the probability space changes dynamically as the neural network learns, a fixed learning rate is incredibly unstable. In some states, a specific \alpha might result in a tiny, inefficient step. In other states, that exact same \alpha might result in a massive jump that completely destroys the policy’s behavior. NPG provides no mathematical guarantees against taking a destructive step.

3.2 How TRPO Extends NPG: The Trust Region

TRPO can be viewed as NPG augmented with a mathematically rigorous step-size calculation. It takes the natural gradient direction from NPG but completely replaces the fixed learning rate with a dynamic scalar derived from a hard Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence constraint.

Instead of guessing a safe learning rate \alpha, TRPO asks the user to define \delta, the maximum allowed change in the probability distribution. By enforcing the constraint D_{KL} \le \delta, TRPO analytically calculates the exact maximum step size that can be taken in the natural gradient direction without violating the trust region:

\Delta \theta = \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{x^T F x}} x

3.3 The Line Search Guarantee

Furthermore, because both the natural gradient and the analytical step size rely on local Taylor approximations, TRPO adds a backtracking line search, which NPG lacks. The line search evaluates the true, unapproximated non-linear objective function and the exact KL divergence to ensure the proposed natural gradient step is strictly beneficial.

In summary, NPG defines the mathematically optimal direction to update a probability distribution, while TRPO provides the mechanical constraints and safety checks required to stably walk in that direction when using deep neural networks.

4 Second-Order Taylor Expansion of the KL Divergence

To derive this approximation, we need to perform a Taylor expansion of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence function around the current policy parameters \theta_{old}.

For clarity in the derivation, let p_\theta(x) represent our parameterized probability distribution, which corresponds to the policy \pi_\theta(a|s) in reinforcement learning.

The KL divergence between the old distribution and the new distribution is defined as:

D_{KL}(p_{\theta_{old}} \parallel p_\theta) = \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \log \frac{p_{\theta_{old}}(x)}{p_\theta(x)}

Let f(\theta) = D_{KL}(p_{\theta_{old}} \parallel p_\theta). We want to approximate f(\theta) at a point \theta = \theta_{old} + \Delta\theta.

The general formula for a second-order multivariate Taylor expansion of f(\theta) around \theta_{old} is:

f(\theta) \approx f(\theta_{old}) + \nabla_\theta f(\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}}^T \Delta\theta + \frac{1}{2} \Delta\theta^T \nabla^2_\theta f(\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}} \Delta\theta

We will evaluate each of these three terms individually.

The zeroth-order term is simply the function evaluated at \theta_{old}:

f(\theta_{old}) = D_{KL}(p_{\theta_{old}} \parallel p_{\theta_{old}})

By definition, the KL divergence between two identical distributions is exactly zero. Mathematically:

D_{KL}(p_{\theta_{old}} \parallel p_{\theta_{old}}) = \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \log \frac{p_{\theta_{old}}(x)}{p_{\theta_{old}}(x)} = \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \log(1) = 0

Next, we calculate the gradient of the KL divergence with respect to the new parameters \theta:

\nabla_\theta f(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \left( \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \left( \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x) - \log p_\theta(x) \right) \right)

Because p_{\theta_{old}}(x) is a constant with respect to \theta, its derivative is zero. We can move the gradient operator inside the summation:

\nabla_\theta f(\theta) = - \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)

Here, we apply the log-derivative trick, which states that \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = \frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)}:

\nabla_\theta f(\theta) = - \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)}

Now, we must evaluate this gradient at the point \theta = \theta_{old} to find the first-order coefficient for our Taylor expansion:

\nabla_\theta f(\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}} = - \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}}}{p_{\theta_{old}}(x)}

The p_{\theta_{old}}(x) terms in the numerator and denominator cancel out:

\nabla_\theta f(\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}} = - \sum_x \nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}}

Because the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, we can pull the gradient operator outside the summation:

\nabla_\theta f(\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}} = - \nabla_\theta \left( \sum_x p_\theta(x) \right)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}}

A fundamental property of any valid probability distribution is that all its probabilities must sum to 1. Therefore, \sum_x p_\theta(x) = 1.

\nabla_\theta f(\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}} = - \nabla_\theta (1) = 0

Because the derivative of a constant is zero, the entire first-order term vanishes.

Since the zeroth-order and first-order terms are both exactly zero, the Taylor expansion relies entirely on the second-order term.

Let H denote the Hessian matrix (the matrix of second derivatives) of the KL divergence evaluated at \theta_{old}:

H = \nabla^2_\theta f(\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_{old}}

Substituting H into the second-order term of the Taylor polynomial gives us the final approximation:

D_{KL}(p_{\theta_{old}} \parallel p_\theta) \approx 0 + 0 + \frac{1}{2} \Delta\theta^T H \Delta\theta

This confirms that for small updates, the KL divergence is approximated purely by a quadratic penalty scaled by the Hessian matrix.

5 Why H = F

Before evaluating it at \theta_{old}, the generalized first derivative of the KL divergence was:

\nabla_\theta D_{KL}(p_{\theta_{old}} \parallel p_\theta) = - \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)

To find the Hessian matrix H, we must take the derivative of this gradient with respect to \theta a second time.

We apply the gradient operator \nabla_\theta to our first derivative. Because p_{\theta_{old}}(x) is independent of \theta, it acts as a constant, allowing us to move the derivative directly to the log-probability term:

H = \nabla^2_\theta D_{KL}(p_{\theta_{old}} \parallel p_\theta) = - \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \nabla^2_\theta \log p_\theta(x)

Now we need to unpack the term \nabla^2_\theta \log p_\theta(x). From the log-derivative trick, we know the first derivative is:

\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = \frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)}

To find the second derivative, we apply the quotient rule to this fraction. For a multivariate function, this expands to:

\nabla^2_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = \frac{\nabla^2_\theta p_\theta(x) p_\theta(x) - \nabla_\theta p_\theta(x) \nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)^T}{p_\theta(x)^2}

We can split this fraction into two separate terms:

\nabla^2_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = \frac{\nabla^2_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)} - \left( \frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)} \right) \left( \frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)} \right)^T

Notice that the second term is just the first derivative of the log-probability multiplied by its own transpose. We can substitute the log-derivative trick back in to simplify:

\nabla^2_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = \frac{\nabla^2_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)} - \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)^T

Now, we substitute this expanded form back into our equation for the Hessian matrix H:

H = - \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \left[ \frac{\nabla^2_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)} - \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)^T \right]

To find the specific Hessian for our Taylor expansion, we must evaluate this entire expression at the point \theta = \theta_{old}.

H = - \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \left[ \frac{\nabla^2_\theta p_{\theta_{old}}(x)}{p_{\theta_{old}}(x)} - \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x)^T \right]

We distribute the summation and the -p_{\theta_{old}}(x) across the two terms inside the brackets.

In the first term, the p_{\theta_{old}}(x) in the numerator and denominator cancel each other out perfectly:

H = - \sum_x \nabla^2_\theta p_{\theta_{old}}(x) + \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x)^T

Let us look closely at the first term: - \sum_x \nabla^2_\theta p_{\theta_{old}}(x). Because the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, we can pull the second derivative operator outside the summation:

- \nabla^2_\theta \left( \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \right)

As established in the previous derivation, the sum of all probabilities in a valid distribution is exactly 1. The second derivative of the constant 1 is zero. Therefore, this entire first term evaluates to zero and disappears.

H = 0 + \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x)^T

We are left with the final, simplified equation for the Hessian of the KL divergence evaluated at the current policy parameters:

H = \sum_x p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \left[ \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x)^T \right]

By definition, a summation over a probability distribution p(x) multiplied by a function of x is the expected value of that function. We can rewrite the equation as an expectation:

H = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_{\theta_{old}}(x)^T \right]

This final expression is the exact mathematical definition of the Fisher Information Matrix F. The term inside the expectation is the outer product of the score function. This completes the proof. It mathematically justifies why TRPO can use the computationally convenient Fisher Information Matrix to define the local geometry of the probability space and satisfy the KL divergence trust-region constraint.
