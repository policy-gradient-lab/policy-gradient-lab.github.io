Multi-step mathematical reasoning is a setting where autoregressive language models frequently fail. A single error early in a solution propagates and is not corrected, so a model that is individually competent at each step can still produce a wrong final answer. The paper addresses this problem with two contributions (Cobbe et al. 2021). The first is GSM8K, a dataset of 8.5K grade-school math word problems with natural-language solutions, designed as a benchmark for informal reasoning. The second is a verification method: instead of generating one solution, the model generates many candidate solutions, and a separately trained verifier scores them so that the highest-scoring candidate can be selected.

1 The GSM8K Dataset

GSM8K consists of 8.5K grade-school math word problems written by human contributors, split into 7.5K training and 1K test problems (Cobbe et al. 2021). Each problem requires between two and eight steps and uses elementary arithmetic operations. Solutions are written in natural language rather than as equations alone.

The dataset is built around four design principles:

  • High quality. Problems are human-written rather than scraped. After agreement checks in which contributors re-solve each other’s problems, the estimated rate of breaking errors is about 1.7%.
  • High diversity. Templated problems are avoided, so problems differ in linguistic form and scenario rather than superficial detail. Held-out accuracy then measures generalization rather than memorization.
  • Moderate difficulty. Difficulty is calibrated to be hard for the largest available models without being intractable, so the dataset can resolve differences in data-scaling between methods.
  • Natural-language solutions. Solutions are collected as natural-language explanations rather than pure expressions.

These properties determine what the benchmark measures. Because problems are diverse and untemplated, held-out accuracy reflects generalization. Because difficulty is calibrated to the capability of large models, the benchmark resolves differences between methods that would be invisible on a dataset that is either saturated or intractable.

To reduce arithmetic mistakes, all models are trained to use a calculator through calculation annotations injected into the training data. At test time, sampling is overridden when the model emits a well-formed annotation, and the expression is evaluated directly.

2 The Finetuning Baseline and Its Scaling Behavior

The baseline method is autoregressive finetuning on the demonstrations, evaluated by a single low-temperature sample per test problem (Cobbe et al. 2021). Across model sizes the test solve rate is approximately log-linear. Extrapolating this trend, attainment of an 80\% solve rate by finetuning alone would require a model of order 10^{16} parameters, or the 175B model trained on at least two additional orders of magnitude of data.

The slope is shallow because autoregressive decoding has no error-correction mechanism. A model writing a multi-step derivation cannot revise an earlier step, so an early error propagates and the probability of a fully correct solution decreases multiplicatively in the number of steps. Raising single-sample accuracy requires reducing the per-step error rate across the entire trajectory.

The intermediate reasoning is necessary, not optional. A 6B model finetuned to emit only the final answer, with no intermediate steps, attains a solve rate of 5.2\%, compared with 20.6\% for the same model when it generates the full natural-language solution (Cobbe et al. 2021).

3 Sampling Coverage: The test@N Metric

Allowing N samples per problem changes the relevant quantity. Define test@N as the fraction of problems for which at least one of N samples is correct. If a single sample solves problem x with probability p_\theta(x) and the samples are independent, then

\text{test@}N \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\, 1 - \big(1 - p_\theta(x)\big)^{N} \,\right].

The expression is written as the complement of joint failure because “at least one of N samples is correct” is the negation of “all N samples are incorrect,” and under independence the probability that all fail is (1 - p_\theta)^N. This form makes the effect of N explicit. A problem solved with per-sample probability 0.05 is solved at least once across 100 samples with probability 1 - 0.95^{100} \approx 0.994. A correct solution is therefore usually present in the sample set even when the generator does not assign it the highest probability on a single greedy draw. This difference between producing a correct solution and producing one as the single most probable output is what the verification method exploits.

The independence assumption is an idealization, and the paper relies on its consequences without stating it. Samples drawn from one model at a fixed temperature are correlated. A model whose output distribution has concentrated retains a high per-sample p_\theta but generates few distinct solutions, so its effective sample count is below N. The training procedure that controls this concentration is discussed below.

4 The Verification Pipeline

The verification method has three steps (Cobbe et al. 2021):

  1. Finetune a generator on the training set for two epochs.
  2. Sample 100 completions per training problem and label each as correct or incorrect according solely to whether its final answer matches.
  3. Train a verifier for one epoch to predict this correctness label.

At test time, the generator produces multiple completions, the verifier scores each, and the highest-scoring completion is returned. The labels are produced by arithmetic comparison of the final answer rather than by human annotation, which is what allows the verifier’s training data to scale.

The two-epoch limit on generator training follows from the coverage metric. Extended generator training increases single-sample accuracy, but test@100 peaks within the first few epochs and then declines as the output distribution concentrates and sample diversity falls (Cobbe et al. 2021). The verifier selects only among supplied candidates, so it requires a generator with high coverage rather than high single-sample accuracy. Stopping generator training early preserves the count of distinct samples on which selection depends.

The relative size of the two models is also informative. A large generator paired with a small verifier outperforms a small generator paired with a large verifier (Cobbe et al. 2021). Candidate coverage contributes more to final accuracy than verifier capacity. A verifier substantially smaller than its generator remains effective, which indicates that it is not performing complete step-by-step verification — a small model lacks the capacity for that — but is instead using shallow features that correlate with correct answers. This property is relevant to the test-time behavior described below.

5 Token-Level and Solution-Level Verifiers

The default verifier emits a prediction after each token rather than a single prediction for the completed solution:

V\big(x,\, y_{\le t}\big) \;\approx\; P\big(\text{final answer correct} \mid x,\, y_{\le t}\big).

This token-level form is a value function over the partial solution. It trains more slowly and with more noise than the solution-level variant, which scores only the completed answer, but it reaches higher final performance and continues to improve while the solution-level variant overfits (Cobbe et al. 2021). Predicting at every prefix requires the model to evaluate the solution as it develops rather than only at the final answer.

The supervision is outcome-based. Each token in a solution receives the single label of final-answer correctness, applied to all positions. Under the standard taxonomy this verifier is an outcome reward model; it is the reference instance against which later process-supervised methods are compared. A process reward model is defined by training on per-step labels that distinguish correct from incorrect steps within a solution, including solutions that reach the correct final answer. This verifier has no such labels, so the token-level prediction adds density of output rather than step-level supervision. Later work supplies the missing labels directly: Lightman et al. train a process reward model on human step-level annotations and release PRM800K, a dataset of 800,000 such labels, reporting that process supervision outperforms outcome supervision on the MATH dataset (Lightman et al. 2023).

This supervision carries a known limitation. A solution that reaches the correct final answer through incorrect reasoning is labeled correct, which produces false positives that per-token prediction cannot resolve, because the labels do not encode step correctness. The verifier is also trained with an auxiliary language-modeling objective alongside the correctness objective; the paper reports this as a consistent improvement, attributable to better modeling of the solution distribution.

6 Test-Time Sample Count and Voting

At test time the selection rule is

\hat{y} \;=\; \arg\max_{i \in \{1,\dots,N\}} V\big(x,\, y_i\big),

the highest-scoring of N samples. The achievable accuracy is bounded above by test@N, because the verifier cannot select a correct solution that was never sampled, and the verifier determines how much of that bound is realized. For moderate N, increasing N raises both the bound and the realized accuracy.

The relation does not hold for large N. Increasing the number of completions improves accuracy up to approximately 400 per problem, after which accuracy declines (Cobbe et al. 2021). The paper attributes this to the selection of “adversarial solutions that fool the verifier”: because the verifier is an estimator of correctness rather than correctness itself, searching over more samples eventually selects solutions that the verifier scores highly but that are not correct. This behavior was later described in terms of reward over-optimization; the paper documents the phenomenon without using that terminology.

One mitigation is to take a majority vote over the highest-ranked candidates rather than selecting the single maximum. With 100 samples, a vote over the top 3–5 candidates is approximately optimal; with 3,200 samples, a vote over the top 30 (Cobbe et al. 2021). A single high-scoring incorrect solution can attain the maximum verifier score but rarely forms a plurality of final answers, so aggregating over several high-ranked candidates reduces sensitivity to the verifier’s largest single error.

7 Regularization with Dropout

Both finetuning and verification benefit from residual dropout, applied at a rate of 20% (Cobbe et al. 2021). The largest effect is on the solution-level verifier, where dropout reduces overfitting to approximately the performance of the token-level verifier. The improvement is smaller for the token-level verifier, which overfits less to begin with. Across the paper’s results, the recurring failure mode is concentration of the model on the single correct answer with loss of the diversity that selection requires, and each technique — the two-epoch generator, the token-level value function, and dropout — counteracts it.

8 Putting It Together

The paper introduces GSM8K and a verification method for grade-school math reasoning. Finetuning a generator and evaluating a single sample improves only slowly with data and model size. The verification method instead generates many candidate solutions and selects among them with a learned verifier, which is trained on outcome labels derived automatically from final-answer correctness. The token-level verifier is an outcome reward model that predicts correctness after each token. On the full dataset, 6B verification slightly exceeds a finetuned 175B model, a difference the paper characterizes as approximately a 30x increase in model size, and verification improves with data faster than the finetuning baseline.

The method also has measurable limits. Accuracy from additional test-time samples increases only up to a point, beyond which selection begins to favor solutions that the verifier scores highly without being correct. The combination of a supervised generator, a learned verifier, and selection over many samples at test time recurs in later work on reasoning systems (DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025; Shao et al. 2024).
