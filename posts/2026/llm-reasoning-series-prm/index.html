A reward model that ranks candidate solutions is only as reliable as the labels it was trained on. For multi-step mathematical reasoning there are two ways to supply those labels. Outcome supervision provides a single label per solution, derived from whether the final answer is correct. Process supervision provides a label for each intermediate step. The paper compares the reward models these two regimes produce, on solutions to problems from the MATH dataset, and finds that process supervision yields the stronger model (Lightman et al. 2023). The best process-supervised reward model selects a correct solution for 78.2\% of a representative MATH test subset under best-of-1860 search, against 72.4\% for the outcome-supervised baseline and 69.6\% for majority voting.

The comparison matters because a single logical error is enough to invalidate a long derivation, and a reward model used for search must detect such errors. Earlier work compared the two supervision regimes on grade-school math and found similar final-answer accuracy, with process supervision reaching it using less data (Cobbe et al. 2021). This paper changes three variables — a more capable base model, substantially more human feedback, and the harder MATH dataset — and reaches a separation between the regimes rather than parity. Alongside the result it releases PRM800K, the full set of 800,000 step-level human labels used to train the best reward model.

1 Two Reward Models, Two Label Sources

The object of study is the reward model, not the generator. A single fixed model, the generator, produces all candidate solutions in a newline-delimited step-by-step format; it is not trained with reinforcement learning. Both reward models are trained on solutions from this generator and differ only in the labels they receive.

The outcome-supervised reward model (ORM) is trained as in the token-level verifiers of the predecessor work (Cobbe et al. 2021). The training set is a fixed number of uniformly sampled solutions per problem — 100 at large scale — each labeled correct or incorrect by automatically checking the final answer. The model predicts this label at every token; the prediction at the final token is taken as the solution score at test time. The automatic grading is the source of the regime’s central weakness: a solution that reaches the correct answer through incorrect reasoning is labeled correct, a false positive the ORM cannot distinguish from a genuinely correct solution.

The process-supervised reward model (PRM) is trained on per-step labels collected from human data-labelers, who mark each step positive, negative, or neutral. A positive step is correct and advances the solution; a negative step is incorrect or unreasonable; a neutral step is ambiguous — correct but subtly misleading, or a valid but poor suggestion. The neutral category exists to defer a decision: at test time a neutral label can be treated as either positive or negative, and the choice is made empirically rather than during annotation. The PRM is trained to predict these labels by maximizing the log-likelihood of the target label token after the last token of each step, which keeps training inside a standard language-model pipeline with no special machinery.

Process supervision is provided only up to the first incorrect step. This is a deliberate design choice, and the reason is that it makes the comparison between the regimes fair rather than favorable to process supervision. On a correct solution both regimes convey the same information: every step is correct. On an incorrect solution both reveal that at least one error exists, and process supervision additionally reveals where the first error is — but no further. Supervising past the first error would widen the information gap and also raise the human labeling cost, because without a checkable final answer, judging a full solution’s correctness reduces to finding its first mistake. Stopping at the first error holds the labeling cost of the two regimes approximately equal, so the comparison isolates the value of step-level location, not the value of more labels.

2 Scoring a Solution from Step Probabilities

A reward model used for best-of-N search must reduce a solution to one comparable score. For the ORM this is the final-token prediction. For the PRM, which emits a correctness probability p_i for each of the K steps, the per-step scores must be combined. The paper defines the solution score as the probability that every step is correct, implemented as the product of the step probabilities:

s(\text{solution}) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{K} p_i .

Why the product? The product is the joint probability that all steps are correct when the step events are treated as independent. This matches the quantity a verifier should report for search: a solution is useful only if it is correct from start to finish, so its score should fall as soon as any step is doubtful. The natural alternative is the minimum, \min_i p_i, which scores a solution by its single weakest step. The paper evaluates both reductions, crossed with the two readings of the neutral label, and reports best-of-1860 test accuracy of 78.2\% for product with neutrals counted positive, 77.6\% for minimum with neutrals positive, 77.4\% and 77.8\% for the two negative-neutral variants. The differences are small; product with neutral-as-positive is selected.

The product reduction carries a structural property the paper notes explicitly: because every factor is at most one, appending more steps can only lower the score, so the product is slightly biased against solutions with more steps. The minimum does not share this bias, since it depends on a single step regardless of length. The near-equal accuracies indicate that this length bias does not materially affect ranking on MATH, but it is the kind of artifact that becomes relevant when solutions vary widely in step count.

3 Best-of-N as the Measure of Reliability

A reward model is evaluated by best-of-N search over uniform samples from the generator. For each test problem the N sampled solutions are scored, the highest-scoring one is selected, and it is graded by its final answer:

\hat{y} \;=\; \arg\max_{i \in \{1,\dots,N\}} s\big(x,\, y_i\big).

The fraction of problems for which \hat{y} is correct is the reported metric. This metric is chosen over training the generator with reinforcement learning because it isolates the reward model: the generator is fixed, so any difference in the reported accuracy is attributable to the reward model’s ability to rank a correct solution above its competitors. A model that ranks correct solutions highest more often is the more reliable one, by definition of the task.

At large scale the PRM exceeds both the ORM and the majority-voting baseline at every value of N, and the gap widens as N grows. The widening is the informative part. Majority voting aggregates final answers and is a strong baseline because a correct answer is often the plurality, but it ignores the reasoning entirely. As N increases the sample set contains more convincing wrong-answer solutions, and a reward model that scores by surface plausibility will increasingly select them. That the PRM’s lead grows with N indicates it continues to separate correct from incorrect reasoning as the pool of distractors expands, where the ORM and majority voting do not.

4 Isolating the Comparison with Synthetic Supervision

The large-scale result is confounded. The PRM and ORM training sets are not comparable: the PRM set was built with active learning, is biased toward wrong-answer solutions, and is an order of magnitude smaller, while the ORM set is 100 uniform samples per problem with no overlap. And the ORM’s final-answer grading admits false positives that may depress its performance — an effect one might or might not want to charge to outcome supervision as such. Collecting human labels to ablate these factors is too costly, so the paper substitutes a synthetic labeler: the large-scale PRM, denoted \text{PRM}_{\text{large}}, supervises a series of smaller reward models on identical datasets, with supervision form the only variable.

Three supervision signals are compared. Process supervision returns \text{PRM}_{\text{large}}’s step labels up to the first negative step, mimicking the human protocol. Outcome supervision from \text{PRM}_{\text{large}} marks a solution correct only if \text{PRM}_{\text{large}} judges every step correct. Outcome supervision from final-answer checking uses the automatic grader. Converting \text{PRM}_{\text{large}}’s probabilities into discrete step labels requires a threshold: a step is called incorrect when \text{PRM}_{\text{large}} assigns its negative label more than 20\% probability. The threshold is set below 50\% deliberately, because \text{PRM}_{\text{large}} is miscalibrated toward positive labels; a symmetric 50\% cut would inherit that bias and under-report errors, so the lower cut compensates for the known calibration skew.

With these controls, process supervision outperforms both outcome signals at every data-collection scale. Within the two outcome baselines, \text{PRM}_{\text{large}} supervision beats final-answer checking, because it supplies better labels to solutions that reach the correct answer through incorrect reasoning — exactly the false-positive case. The paper declines to declare one outcome baseline canonical: final-answer checking is more purely outcome-based, but its false-positive weakness is arguably over-represented on MATH, whereas \text{PRM}_{\text{large}} supervision better models outcome supervision in domains less prone to false positives. It treats the latter as the more relevant baseline while leaving the reader to weigh the choice. The comparison’s significance is that process supervision wins even when the outcome labels are made clean, which removes the false-positive explanation and isolates the advantage to step-level credit.

5 Active Learning and the Cost of Labels

Process supervision requires human labels, so its practical viability depends on label efficiency. The data-collection strategy surfaces the solutions most informative to label, which the paper operationalizes as convincing wrong-answer solutions: convincing meaning highly rated by the current best PRM, wrong-answer meaning the final answer is incorrect. Such a solution is known to contain at least one error the PRM has failed to catch, so labeling it is guaranteed to correct a model mistake, whereas a uniformly sampled solution is often correct or obviously wrong and teaches little.

The synthetic setup permits a controlled measurement. A small reward model \text{PRM}_{\text{selector}}, trained on one sample per problem, scores 1000 samples per problem; for each larger model, N samples per problem are selected so that 80\% are the most convincing wrong-answer samples under \text{PRM}_{\text{selector}} and 20\% are the most convincing remaining samples, correct or not. The 20\% admixture keeps the dataset from collapsing entirely onto wrong answers while preserving the property that most samples contain an error. Comparing the slope of accuracy against dataset size with and without this selection, active learning is approximately 2.6\times more data-efficient than uniform sampling.

The estimate has a documented boundary. The model trained on the largest active-learning dataset, 200 samples per problem, falls slightly below the trend, which the paper attributes to 200 being a large fraction of the 1000-sample selection pool, leaving too little diversity for selection to exploit. A preliminary attempt to retrain \text{PRM}_{\text{selector}} iteratively during collection produced instability the authors could not diagnose and no improvement, so the reported gain comes from a single fixed selector.

6 Generalization and the Source of the Advantage

To test whether the PRM’s advantage is specific to the MATH distribution, both large-scale models are evaluated on recent STEM exam questions — AP Calculus, AP Chemistry, AP Physics, and AMC10/12 — released after the pretraining corpus was assembled, so they cannot be contaminated. Across 234 problems in aggregate, best-of-100 selection gives the PRM 72.9\% against the ORM’s 63.8\% and majority voting’s 61.3\%, with the PRM ahead in every subject. The ordering observed on MATH holds on guaranteed-fresh problems, which both supports generalization and weakens the possibility that test-set contamination drives the main result.

The mechanism the paper offers for the advantage is credit assignment. An outcome-supervised model receiving a single negative label on a hard problem must infer which step was responsible, and on hard problems nearly every sampled solution contains an error, so the marginal information in one more negative outcome label is low. Process supervision states how many leading steps were correct and where the first error is, which is a denser signal and removes the inference. The paper presents this as the explanation for the performance gap, consistent with the synthetic result that the advantage survives after the false-positive confound is removed.

A second consequence concerns alignment. Safer training methods often reduce capability, a cost termed an alignment tax, and such a tax discourages adoption. Process supervision rewards a human-endorsed chain of reasoning directly rather than using the outcome as a proxy, which is more interpretable and less exposed to a model that learns to exploit an outcome proxy. Because process supervision is also the stronger method here, the paper characterizes its alignment tax as negative — the more interpretable signal is also the more accurate one — and argues this alignment of incentives could drive adoption. The scope of that claim is explicitly limited to mathematical reasoning; whether it extends to domains without cheaply checkable answers is left open, and in such domains the false-positive weakness of outcome supervision that makes MATH favorable to process supervision may itself behave differently.

7 Where This Leads

The paper’s result is that a reward model trained to score each reasoning step ranks correct MATH solutions above incorrect ones more reliably than one trained on final-answer correctness, and that the gap is not an artifact of unequal training sets or final-answer false positives, since it persists under controlled synthetic supervision with clean outcome labels. The mechanism is denser credit assignment: a step-level label tells the model where reasoning fails, which an outcome label does not. Active learning, by surfacing convincing wrong-answer solutions, makes the human labeling that process supervision requires roughly 2.6\times more efficient, and the released PRM800K dataset supplies the step-level labels for further work. The structure — a fixed generator, a learned reward model, and selection over many samples — extends the verifier method of the predecessor work (Cobbe et al. 2021). Whether step-level supervision is worth its labeling cost is a question later work answers in both directions: this paper argues the step-level signal is decisive on MATH, while some subsequent reasoning systems forgo a process reward model entirely in favor of outcome-based rule rewards, reporting that a learned step-scorer is hard to define and prone to reward hacking at scale (DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025).
